How a tankless heater differs from a tank

A tank heater keeps dozens of gallons hot all day. A tankless unit heats water only when a tap opens, so it never runs out as long as the demand stays within its flow rating. That is why households with several bathrooms and heavy morning use often like them.

The trade-off is the setup. A tankless gas unit needs a bigger gas line, a vent rated for its exhaust and a condensate drain on high-efficiency models. Those requirements decide whether it is a simple swap or a bigger project, and we check them before quoting.

Tankless installation: what we check first

Gas supply

The meter, line size and pressure must support the unit's full input rating.

Venting

Exhaust runs through sealed plastic or stainless pipe to the outside, with clearances from windows and openings.

Water flow

We compare the unit's flow rate with how many fixtures run together.

Condensate drain

High-efficiency models make acidic condensate that needs a neutralizer and a drain.

Older Plainfield homes can be tricky because the gas line may be sized for a small tank heater and a boiler. If the line is too small, we tell you what an upgrade involves before you decide.

Tankless repair and the error codes behind it

Most tankless problems come down to a short list. The unit shows an error code, runs lukewarm, shuts off mid-shower or cycles between hot and cold. These point to scale on the heat exchanger, a clogged inlet filter, a vent problem or a failing sensor.

We read the code, check the filter and flow, and test the ignition and sensors. If scale is the cause, a flush usually restores the unit. If a part has failed, we replace it with the correct manufacturer part and check the vent while we are there.

Flush and descale: the yearly job people skip

Hard minerals in the water build up on the heat exchanger and reduce flow and efficiency. Flushing the unit with a descaling solution through a pump clears that buildup. Manufacturers generally recommend doing it on a regular schedule, and the right interval depends on your water.

We do not quote a hardness figure for your house unless it comes from a source we can name. If you want a number, New Jersey American Water publishes water quality information for its service areas, and a simple test kit shows what comes out of your own tap. Where hardness is high, a softener upstream of the heater can also protect it. Ask us about it on the visit.

Plainfield note: gas and permits

The City of Plainfield's homeowner manual says installing a hot water heater requires a permit, and the Construction Division at 908-226-2665 can tell you how that applies to a tankless unit. Because a tankless install usually touches gas piping and venting, we plan the permit and inspection into the schedule.

Code and manufacturer rules

A tankless install must meet the Plumbing Subcode, N.J.A.C. 5:23-3.15, and the maker's instructions. Gas tankless units often need a larger gas line and special venting, and the manufacturer lists the exact requirements for each model.
