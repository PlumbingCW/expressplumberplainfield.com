Water Leak Detection in Your House: Plainfield, NJ Guide

Hidden leaks waste water, damage framing and run up bills. This guide shows how to check for one with a meter test and a few simple steps, and when it is time for equipment.

By the Plainfield Express Plumbing team · Published November 13, 2026.

Signs you may have a hidden leak

A higher bill

Your usage rises without a change in habits.

The sound of running water

You hear it when everything is off.

Stains and bubbling paint

Ceilings, walls or baseboards show discoloration.

Musty smell

A damp odor in a basement, closet or cabinet.

One sign does not prove a leak. Together, or repeated over time, they usually do.

The meter test

  1. Turn off every fixture. Make sure no appliance is running. Ice makers and humidifiers count.
  2. Find the meter. It is usually in the basement near where the line enters, or in a pit near the street.
  3. Read the dial or the leak indicator. Many meters have a small triangle or dial that spins with any flow.
  4. Wait fifteen minutes and read again. If the reading changed, water is leaving the house.
  5. Close the main valve and check again. If the meter stops, the leak is inside. If it keeps moving, suspect the line before the valve.

Check the usual suspects

PlaceHow to test
Toilets.Add food coloring to the tank. If color appears in the bowl without flushing, the flapper leaks.
Faucets and shutoff valves.Look for drips and damp supply lines under sinks.
Water heater.Look for moisture at the base and around fittings.

Outdoor hose bibs.Look for drips and damp soil near the wall.

Toilets cause a surprising share of hidden water waste, and the fix is cheap. See toilet repair if you find a leaking flapper.

When to bring in a professional

If the meter moves and you cannot find the source, the leak may be in a wall, a ceiling, under a slab or in the buried service line. Those need equipment. Acoustic listening picks up the sound of water escaping, thermal imaging shows temperature differences and moisture meters map wet material. Our leak detection page explains how we use each.

Do not open a wall until the leak has been located. A wet spot on drywall can sit feet away from the source, because water travels along framing.

Where hidden leaks start

Leaks seldom begin in the middle of a pipe. They start at the points where a pipe changes: fittings, valves, fixtures and appliance connections. Look at those places first.

Under sinks

Supply lines, angle stops and drain connections. A damp cabinet floor is the clue.

Behind toilets

Supply lines and the wax ring. Soft flooring near the base is the clue.

At appliances

Washer hoses, dishwasher lines and ice maker tubes.

In the basement ceiling

Joints on supply and drain pipes above the finished space.

A moisture meter and a flashlight are cheap tools that find many leaks. Run your hand along pipes and feel for dampness. Check the underside of the floor above a bathroom for staining.

Slab and under-house leaks

Leaks under a slab or in a crawl space are harder to spot. Warm patches on a floor, the sound of running water at night and a spinning meter with everything off all point that way. The pipes are not visible, so equipment is the way to find the spot. A pressure test of each line can narrow the section before any listening begins.

Preventing the next leak

Prevention is mostly routine. Replace rubber washing machine hoses with braided ones every few years. Test shutoff valves each year by closing and reopening them. Keep the area under sinks clear so you notice dampness. Know your normal water pressure, because high pressure stresses every joint in the house. A pressure gauge on an outdoor spigot takes a minute to read and is one of the cheapest checks you can do.

Finally, if you are away in winter, set the heat to a safe level and ask someone to check the house. A burst pipe in an empty house can run for days.

Keeping records for an insurer

If a leak has caused damage, evidence matters. Take dated photos of the source, the damage and the meter reading. Keep receipts for repairs, drying equipment and any temporary housing. Write down the date you first noticed the problem and the date you shut off the water.

Insurers often distinguish between sudden leaks and long-term seepage. A record that shows the leak was found and fixed promptly supports your claim. A plumber's written note on the cause and the repair adds to it. Ask for one when the work is done.

Plainfield note: meters, the utility and the City manual

Water in Plainfield comes from New Jersey American Water, and the meter is the utility's, so questions about the meter reading go to them. The City of Plainfield's homeowner manual tells residents to check pipe joints for leaks and repair them immediately, to run faucets regularly and to check all shutoff valves once a year to make sure they work.

The manual also warns that water seeping behind missing bathroom tile or under a poorly caulked tub can cause damage to walls and floors. That kind of slow leak is common in older houses and often goes unnoticed.

What to do when you find one

  1. Shut off the nearest valve, or the main if the source is unclear.
  2. Photograph the damage and the meter reading.
  3. Dry the area quickly to limit mold.
  4. Get the leak located and repaired by a licensed plumber.
  5. Tell your insurer if there is damage.

A leak found early is a small repair. A leak found after months can mean framing, insulation and drywall. For old supply pipe with repeated leaks, repiping may be worth comparing. We serve Plainfield, North Plainfield and Piscataway.
