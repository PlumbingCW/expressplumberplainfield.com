Tankless Water Heater Venting in Plainfield, NJ

Venting decides whether a tankless water heater is a simple swap or a bigger project. This guide explains the vent types, the clearances to ask about and the questions an older Plainfield house raises.

By the Plainfield Express Plumbing team · Published November 13, 2026.

Why venting comes first

A gas tankless heater burns a lot of fuel in a small space, and it produces exhaust that must leave the building safely. The vent is how it does that. If the vent is wrong, the heater can shut down, run poorly or, in the worst case, release carbon monoxide into the house.

That makes the vent the first thing to settle, not an afterthought. It decides where the heater can go, what it costs to install and which models fit.

The main vent types

TypeHow it worksWhere it fits
Direct vent, two pipes.One pipe brings in outside air, one sends exhaust out.Sealed combustion, good for tight spaces.
Concentric vent.A pipe within a pipe, one run through the wall.Tidy wall exits with a single penetration.
Power vent, single pipe.Draws room air and pushes exhaust out.Needs enough combustion air in the room.
Existing chimney with a liner.Exhaust runs up a relined flue.Possible, but only if the liner is sized for it.

Most modern tankless heaters are condensing units. Because they extract so much heat, the exhaust is cool enough to run through plastic pipe, and it produces acidic condensate that must drain away. Always follow the manufacturer's venting instructions and the code.

Clearances and termination

The vent outlet cannot be placed just anywhere. It needs set distances from windows, doors, air intakes, gas meters and the property line, and it has to clear snow in winter. Wind and neighboring buildings matter too. The rules come from the manufacturer's instructions and the fuel gas code, and the local inspector enforces them.

  • Keep the outlet away from operable windows and doors.
  • Mind the distance to a neighbor's wall, especially on narrow lots.
  • Plan for snow cover and drifts at the outlet height.
  • Slope horizontal runs back toward the heater so condensate drains.
  • Support the pipe and seal joints as the manufacturer specifies.

Combustion air and gas supply

If a unit takes air from the room, the room must supply enough of it. A sealed basement closet with a tankless heater is a recipe for poor combustion. Direct-vent units avoid the issue by bringing air in through a dedicated pipe.

The gas line also matters. A tankless heater draws a lot more input than a tank burner, and the line, the meter and the regulator all have to keep up. See our tankless water heater page for what we check. A line that is too small starves the unit, and the heater cuts out under heavy demand.

Condensate: the quiet part of a vent job

A condensing tankless heater pulls so much heat from the exhaust that water vapor turns to liquid inside the unit. That condensate is mildly acidic. It has to drain to a floor drain, a laundry sink or a condensate pump, often through a neutralizer cartridge that raises the pH before it reaches the drain.

A neutralizer needs checking once a year and replacement on schedule. A clogged condensate line can back up into the heat exchanger and shut the unit down with an error. Many service calls on new tankless units trace to this line, not to the heater itself.

What a good installer shows you

Before the job, expect a drawing or a clear explanation of the vent route, the termination point and the condensate path. After the job, expect a combustion check, a tour of the vent and a note of the filter and neutralizer locations. If an installer cannot explain the vent route in plain words, ask again. It is the part of the job that most affects safety.

Inspection day

When the inspector arrives, have the manual, the permit and the model number ready. They will look at the vent route, the clearances, the gas connection and the condensate drain. Fix anything they flag and ask for a re-inspection. Keep the approval paperwork with your house records, because it helps when you sell.

Common venting mistakes

  • Using the wrong pipe material for the unit.
  • Skipping the slope on horizontal runs, so condensate pools.
  • Placing the outlet too close to a window or neighbor's wall.
  • Letting the vent terminate where snow can bury it.
  • Mixing parts from different manufacturers' systems.
  • Forgetting to support long runs, so joints sag and leak.

Each of these is avoidable with planning. If an installer proposes a route that looks improvised, ask them to check the manufacturer's venting chart for your model. The chart lists the allowed pipe, the maximum length and the number of elbows, and any deviation should be a conscious choice that can be defended to the inspector.

Plainfield note: old chimneys and permits

Many Plainfield houses date from the late 1800s and early 1900s, including the Crescent Area Historic District with 111 contributing buildings, most built between 1870 and 1900. Those houses often have a masonry chimney sized for a coal or oil boiler. A modern, smaller flue gas stream can condense in an oversized chimney and damage the masonry, so the chimney is usually not reused for a condensing tankless heater.

The City of Plainfield's homeowner manual lists installing a hot water heater, and changing the heating system to a different type, as work that needs a permit. The Construction Division at 908-226-2665 can tell you what applies, and a vent route is part of what the inspector will want to see.

Questions to ask before you buy

  1. Where will the vent exit and does it meet the clearances?
  2. Is the existing chimney reusable, or does the heater need its own vent?
  3. Is the gas line large enough for the unit's full input?
  4. Where will condensate drain and is a neutralizer needed?
  5. Does the permit cover the vent and the gas piping?
