Whole House Tankless Water Heater for Plainfield, NJ Homes

A whole house tankless heater can supply hot water to the entire home without a storage tank. Whether it fits depends on flow, gas capacity and how your household uses water. This guide shows how to size one and what to expect.

By the Plainfield Express Plumbing team · Published November 13, 2026.

What whole house means

A single tankless unit sized to serve every fixture in the house is called a whole house system. It heats water on demand as it passes through the unit, instead of heating a tank around the clock. A smaller point-of-use unit serves one sink or shower. A whole house unit has to handle several demands at once.

The key idea is flow, not volume. A tank is limited by how much hot water it holds. A tankless heater is limited by how many gallons per minute it can heat. Once the demand passes that limit, the water runs lukewarm.

How to size a tankless heater

  1. List the fixtures you use at the same time. Think of the busiest morning. Two showers and a dishwasher or a washing machine is a common peak.
  2. Add up their flow rates. A shower head commonly uses a couple of gallons a minute, a kitchen faucet a bit more than one and a washer a few. The fixture labels show the numbers.
  3. Find the temperature rise you need. Subtract the incoming water temperature from the temperature you want. Colder incoming water in winter means a bigger rise and a lower flow from the same unit.
  4. Match the unit's rating. Manufacturers publish flow rates at several temperature rises. Choose a unit that meets your winter peak, not your summer one.

Sizing for the coldest month is the part people miss. A heater that handles the demand in July may fall short in January when the water entering the house is much colder.

Most whole house installs in homes with natural gas use gas units. A whole house electric unit pulls so much current that many older panels cannot support it. An electrician can tell you if yours can.

Maintenance you cannot skip

A tankless heater needs a yearly flush to remove scale from the heat exchanger. Without it, efficiency drops and the unit can lock out on an error. Check the inlet screen as well. See tankless water heater for what a service visit includes.

Keep the manual and the model number handy. If the unit shows an error code, the code usually points to the problem, whether scale, a vent restriction or an ignition fault.

Real-world examples of sizing

A couple in a two-bath house who shower at different times rarely stress a mid-size unit. A family of five with teenagers and a dishwasher running during morning showers needs more flow. A house with a large soaking tub that fills while another shower runs may need two units or a larger one.

The goal is not the biggest unit. It is the smallest unit that meets your winter peak. Oversizing raises the installation cost, the gas line requirement and the vent size without improving daily comfort.

Recirculation and wait times

Tankless heaters deliver hot water quickly once they fire, but the pipe between the heater and the faucet is still full of cold water. A recirculation pump keeps hot water moving through a loop so it arrives faster. Some tankless models have a built-in recirculation feature, and others need an external pump.

Winter performance and what to expect

Incoming water gets much colder in winter, so the heater must add more heat to reach the same outlet temperature. Each unit has a maximum temperature rise it can deliver at a given flow. If the demand is high and the water is cold, the outlet temperature falls or the flow is limited.

That is why winter sizing is the correct basis. A household that never runs out of hot water in summer may notice lukewarm showers in January if the unit was sized by a summer calculation. Ask for the winter flow figure before you buy.

Plainfield note: water supply and permits

New Jersey American Water is the utility that lists Plainfield in its service territory. Is a whole house tankless right for you

Good fit

A household with large, simultaneous demand, limited space for a tank and a gas line that can support the unit.

Fit with limits

A small household that wants lower standby loss and has a clear vent path.

Poor fit

A home with an undersized gas line, no vent route or a small electrical panel.

Worth comparing

A tank heater with strong recovery, for a lower upfront cost.
