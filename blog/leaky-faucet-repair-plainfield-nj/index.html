Leaky Faucet Repair: What a Drip Costs in Plainfield, NJ

A slow drip feels harmless, but it runs all day and every day. This guide shows how to measure what a leaky faucet wastes, which tub and bathroom faucets fail in which ways and when the fix is a part or a plumber.

By the Plainfield Express Plumbing team · Published November 13, 2026.

Measure the drip

You can estimate what a drip costs with a measuring cup and a clock. Place a cup under the faucet, time it for ten minutes and note how much water collects. Multiply by the number of ten-minute periods in a day and in a month.

A drip that fills a quarter cup in ten minutes works out to well over a gallon a day. It adds up over weeks and shows on the water bill. It also wears the seat, because dripping water carries minerals that erode metal.

Leaks behind a tub or shower valve do not announce themselves. If you see staining on the ceiling below a bathroom, treat it as urgent and use leak detection to find the source before anyone opens a wall.

Bathroom sink faucets

Bathroom faucets are often smaller and less robust than kitchen ones. A dripping spout usually means a cartridge or washer. A leak at the handle base is a seal. A weak flow with a drip points to mineral buildup in the aerator and the cartridge.

Many bathroom faucets have a pop-up drain linked to a lever behind the spout. A leak under the sink can come from the drain linkage, not the faucet. Check both before you buy parts.

Parts or plumber

A DIY job

A cartridge swap on a standard single-handle faucet with working shutoff valves.

A plumber job

A concealed valve, a stuck shutoff or corroded pipe in the wall.

A replacement job

A faucet with a corroded body or no available parts.

A bigger problem

A leak that follows the supply line, which may need repiping if the pipe is old.

If the shutoff valves under the sink do not stop the water, close the main valve and replace them. See faucet and sink repair for what that visit includes.

Do the math on a drip

Say your faucet fills a cup in an hour. That is about a quart a day, or roughly nine gallons a month. A faster drip, a cup in ten minutes, comes to roughly fifty gallons a month. These are not big numbers on a household scale, but they run all year, and the faucet gets worse as the seat erodes.

A repair that takes an hour with a cheap part is nearly always worth it. A faucet that has dripped for years also stains the basin, which is a sign of mineral buildup nearby.

Handles, finishes and when to upgrade

A faucet is more than its cartridge. If the finish is peeling, if the handle wobbles or if the body is corroded, a new faucet is a better use of money than another repair. Choose a model from a brand that supplies parts for years. A faucet with replaceable cartridges is easier to maintain than one with sealed internals.

Also consider water-saving aerators. They lower the flow without changing the feel much, and they reduce the volume of any future drip.

Single-handle, two-handle and tub-shower types

A single-handle faucet uses one cartridge for both temperature and flow, so a worn cartridge affects both. A two-handle faucet has separate valves, and the dripping one is the one to fix. A tub and shower valve may be a three-handle set or a single pressure-balance valve. Pressure-balance valves protect against scalding when a toilet flushes, so they should be replaced with the same type. A plumber can identify the valve by the trim and the depth of the stem.

When the leak is not the faucet

Not every drip comes from the faucet. A leak under the sink may come from the drain trap, a slip nut or a garbage disposal connection. A drip at the ceiling below a bathroom may come from the tub drain or the overflow gasket, and it only appears when the tub is used. Run each fixture separately and watch where water appears.

A good trick is to dry everything, wrap a dry paper towel around each joint and run the water for a minute. The towel that gets wet first marks the leak. Do this with the stopper in, then out, because the overflow and the drain leak in different conditions.

If you cannot find the source, shut off the supply and see whether the drip stops. A drip that stops with the supply off is a pressure leak on the supply side. One that continues is a drain or a condensation issue.

Plainfield note: water service and bathroom care

Water service in Plainfield comes from New Jersey American Water, and a high bill is worth testing before assuming it is the meter. The City of Plainfield's homeowner manual tells residents to check pipe joints for leaks and repair them immediately and to check all shutoff valves once a year.

The manual also warns that water seeping behind missing bathroom tile or beneath a tub with failing caulk can damage walls and floors. A dripping tub faucet adds water to exactly that spot every day. Fix the faucet and check the caulk at the same time.

A simple repair checklist

  1. Measure the drip so you know what it wastes.
  2. Identify the faucet type and the brand.
  3. Close the shutoff valves and plug the drain.
  4. Replace the cartridge, washer or seal with the exact part.
  5. Test through a full range of hot and cold.
  6. Check the caulk and the area below for moisture.
